Globalisation gets blamed for almost every modern headache: hollowed-out factory
suburbs, flatlining wages, and angry politics. At the same time, mainstream
economists often speak about open trade as an unqualified miracle that lifted
millions out of poverty.

The truth sits in the middle. Global trade creates immense wealth, but it comes
with a catch: the perks are spread thin across millions of shoppers, while the
costs slam into specific suburbs, factories, and working families. To understand
the political backlash tearing through Western democracies, we need to look past
the talking points and audit who actually won, who lost, and what it cost.

## The Economists' Promise

Mainstream economists have championed open trade since [David Ricardo][ricardo]
introduced comparative advantage in 1817. The basic logic is simple: when
countries specialise in what they make best, overall output surges and prices
fall. Bigger markets allow businesses to scale, capital flows to where it works
hardest, and lazy local monopolies are forced to compete.

In the aggregate, this model delivered. Over four decades of export-led growth,
[World Bank][world-bank-poverty] data shows extreme global poverty dropping from
more than a third of the world's population in 1990 to under ten per cent before
2020. Hundreds of millions of people across China and South-East Asia moved from
      subsistence farming into steady, paid work.

Yet economists made one compromised assumption: the standard models assume
workers and capital move effortlessly. If a textile mill closes, the theory says
staff can pack up, retrain, and slide into high-tech jobs. In the real world,
people cannot uproot their families as easily as moving numbers across a
spreadsheet. Theory also assumes the winners will share their gains with the
losers through safety nets and fair taxes. Politics rarely honoured that
bargain.

## The Factory Floor

For blue-collar households, the impact of open borders depended entirely on
where they lived.

In wealthy nations, manufacturing workers took the brunt of the hit. The
landmark _[China Shock][china-shock]_ research by David Autor, David Dorn, and
Gordon Hanson showed how a flood of cheap imports hollowed out industrial
heartlands across the United States, Europe and Australia, displacing roughly
two million workers. When anchor factories closed, whole communities endured
decades of depressed wages and fraying social bonds. The main consolation
arrived at the checkout: cheap clothing, appliances, and electronics helped
modest paycheques stretch further, but low-paid retail jobs never replaced the
security of unionised manufacturing.

In developing nations, the story flipped. Factory work gave millions of families
a genuine ladder out of rural poverty. It brought women into the formal
workforce in record numbers, improving household income and children's health.
Yet that progress came with steep human costs: hazardous workshops, minimal
labour rights, and constant insecurity. Because global brands perpetually hunt
for the lowest price tag, local contracts can vanish overnight if wages rise or
a neighbouring country devalues its currency.

## White-Collar Jobs Are Next

For decades, educated professionals viewed globalisation as an unmitigated win.
Lawyers, consultants, software engineers, and financiers suddenly had the entire
planet as their client base.

Now, competition is climbing the corporate ladder. High-speed internet, cloud
tools, and generative AI mean office jobs are directly exposed to global
rivalry. As economist [Richard Baldwin][baldwin] points out, white-collar
"tele-migrants" have joined the arena. A software developer or financial analyst
in Melbourne, London, or San Francisco is increasingly competing against equally
talented peers in Bangalore, Warsaw, or Manila who charge a fraction of the
cost.

This shift has also hardened politics. For years, metropolitan knowledge workers
seemed indifferent to the collapse of factory towns. That perceived smugness
bred deep resentment toward city elites, eroding the public support that open
borders require.

## The Politics of Losing Control

For national governments, dismantling trade barriers created a painful trade-
off between national wealth and democratic control.

Trade boosts GDP and expands tax revenues, while access to global bond markets
lowers borrowing costs for hospitals and roads. For middle powers like
Australia, multilateral rules from the World Trade Organization provide vital
protection against economic bullying by superpowers.

The hidden price was policy independence. As economist [Dani Rodrik][rodrik]
famously observed, you cannot simultaneously have hyper-globalisation, national
sovereignty, and a responsive democracy. To keep mobile global capital happy,
governments cut corporate tax rates, shifted tax burdens onto ordinary workers,
and signed treaties that restricted their ability to protect local industries.

The fragility of that bargain was exposed by pandemics, energy crunches, and
rivalry between the United States and China. Relying on geopolitical rivals for
medicines, microchips, and critical minerals turned out to be an immense gamble.
Today, industrial policy is back: sovereign capability funds, green
manufacturing subsidies, and _friend-shoring_ prove that supply-chain resilience
now outweighs raw cost-cutting.

## Offshoring the Pollution

We also have to account for the environmental bill. While international commerce
speeds up the rollout of solar panels and batteries, it also enables a shell
game with pollution.

Heavy polluters routinely move production to countries with lax environmental
standards—a dodge known as carbon leakage. While schemes like the European
Union's [Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism][cbam] attempt to penalise these
embedded emissions, massive shipping fleets continue burning dirty bunker fuel,
polluting the oceans and leaving the public to clean up the mess.

## The Cost of Looking Away

Globalisation is not fundamentally evil. Open trade lifted living standards,
offered boundless consumer choice, and powered the sharpest decline in extreme
poverty in human history.

Yet the maths of the political backlash is straightforward: diffuse benefits
cannot soothe concentrated pain. Saving ten dollars on a toaster means nothing
to a family whose factory closed, whose wages stalled for twenty years, and
whose community lost its purpose.

The lesson of the past three decades is not that we should pull up the
drawbridge behind punitive tariff walls. It is that open trade cannot survive on
textbook fantasies. If governments fail to invest in serious retraining, strong
safety nets, and secure domestic supply lines, voters will continue to tear down
the very system that made them prosperous.
