Globalisation gets blamed for almost every modern headache: hollowed-out factory
towns, flatlining wages, regional inequality, and angry politics. At the same
time, mainstream economists often talk about open trade as an unqualified
miracle for lifting millions out of poverty.

The truth sits somewhere in between. Global trade does create immense wealth,
but it comes with a catch: the benefits are spread thin across millions of
shoppers, while the costs slam into specific towns and workers. To make sense of
the backlash, we need to look past the talking points and inspect the ledger
across four groups – economists, blue-collar workers, professionals, and
governments – before checking the environmental bill.

## Economists: Aggregate Efficiency versus Friction

Mainstream economists have long loved trade because of
[David Ricardo's][ricardo] theory of comparative advantage. The basic logic is
simple: when countries focus on what they do best, overall output jumps and
prices fall for everyone. Bigger markets let businesses scale up, direct capital
towards where it works hardest, and keep lazy local monopolies on their toes.

Looking at the big picture, this model delivered. Over the past forty years,
export-driven growth helped hundreds of millions of people across China,
Vietnam, Bangladesh, and South-East Asia move from subsistence farming into
steady, paid work.

Yet economists have had to rethink the fine print. Classic textbook models
assume that moving money and workers is effortless. In theory, if a factory
shuts down, staff can pack up, retrain, and slide straight into high-tech jobs.
In the real world, human beings cannot uproot their lives as easily as moving
numbers across a spreadsheet. Standard models also assume the winners will share
their bounty with the losers through taxes and safety nets – but politics rarely
delivers on that promise. Throw in volatile global capital flows that trigger
sudden currency crashes, and the textbook theory starts looking shaky.

## Ordinary Workers: A Tale of Two Worlds

The impact of open borders on working-class households depends largely on where
they live.

### Developed Economies: The China Shock

In wealthy nations, manufacturing workers took the brunt of the hit. The
landmark research on the _[China Shock][china-shock]_ by David Autor, David
Dorn, and Gordon Hanson showed how a surge of cheap imports hollowed out
industrial heartlands across the United States and parts of Europe.

These scars ran deep. When factories closed, entire towns suffered decades of
joblessness, lower wages, and fraying communities. Government retraining
programmes were often underfunded and out of touch. Worse still, once bosses
could credibly threaten to move production abroad, local unions lost their
bargaining power.

The main silver lining showed up at the checkout: cheaper clothes, shoes, and
electronics helped modest weekly paycheques stretch further. Warehousing,
delivery, and retail jobs grew, but they rarely matched the pay, stability, or
union benefits of the factory floors they replaced.

### Developing Economies: Progress and Precariousness

In developing nations, the story flipped. Factory work gave millions of families
a genuine ladder out of backbreaking farm labour and into reliable wages. It
brought millions of women into the formal workforce for the first time, directly
improving family health and schooling.

Yet that progress came at a steep human cost. Loose labour laws often mean
brutal hours, hazardous workshops, and banned unions. And because global brands
obsess over keeping costs rock-bottom, workers live with constant uncertainty:
if local wages rise or a competitor devalues its currency, multinational
contracts can vanish overnight.

## Professional Workers: The Expanding Horizon and New Frontlines

For decades, educated professionals saw globalisation as a pure win. Anyone
working in software, law, finance, consulting, or medicine suddenly had the
world as their client base. Working across borders – especially with the rise of
remote work – drove professional salaries through the roof.

Now, competition is climbing the corporate ladder. High-speed internet, cloud
platforms, and asynchronous tools mean office jobs – from coding and financial
analysis to reading X-rays – are directly exposed to global competition. A
consultant or software engineer in Sydney, London, or San Francisco is
increasingly competing against equally talented, English-speaking peers in
Bangalore, Warsaw, or Manila who charge a fraction of the price.

This also created bad political blood. For years, metropolitan knowledge workers
seemed indifferent to the pain of fading industrial towns. That perceived
smugness bred deep resentment towards city elites – gradually eroding the
political support that open trade depends on.

## Governments: Sovereignty, Supply Chains, and the Rodrik Trilemma

For national governments, opening borders creates a tricky tug-of-war between
national growth and democratic control.

### The Gains: Growth, Capital, and Multilateral Shielding

The upside for policymakers is clear. Trade drives GDP and boosts tax revenue.
Access to global bond markets helps fund roads, hospitals, and emergency
spending at lower interest rates. And for middle powers like Australia, clear
international trade rules anchored by bodies like the World Trade Organization
offer protection against bullying by superpowers.

### The Costs: Tied Hands and Broken Supply Chains

The trade-off is a loss of control at home. As economist [Dani Rodrik][rodrik]
famously pointed out, you cannot have all three at the same time: hyper-speed
global trade, national independence, and a responsive democracy. You have to
pick two:

* If you want deep global integration and national sovereignty, you have to do
  whatever foreign bond markets and multinational capital want – even if voters
  hate it.
* If you want national sovereignty and real democratic choices, you have to set
  firm boundaries on global trade.
* If you want global integration and democracy, you need a world government,
  giving up national independence entirely.

For thirty years, governments picked the first option. In the race to attract
global money, countries cut company tax rates, shifting the tax bill onto
ordinary workers and shoppers. Meanwhile, strict trade treaties stopped
governments from protecting local industries.

Then reality hit. Pandemics, energy crunches, and growing rivalry between the
United States and China exposed just how fragile 'just-in-time' global supply
chains really are. Relying on geopolitical rivals for life-saving medicines,
microchips, and critical minerals turned out to be a major security gamble.

Now, governments are changing their tune. Subsidies for green manufacturing,
sovereign capability funds, and pushes towards _near-shoring_ and
_friend-shoring_ show a clear change of heart: keeping supply lines secure with
trusted allies now matters more than chasing the lowest price tag.

## The Ecological Bill: Offshoring Pollution

We also need to look at the environmental toll. While global commerce helps
spread solar panels and battery tech around the world, it also lets dirty
industries game the system.

High-emission manufacturers frequently move factories to countries with weak
environmental rules – a dodge known as carbon leakage. On top of that, the
massive shipping fleets powering global commerce burn dirty bunker fuel, pollute
the oceans, and spread invasive species, leaving the public to pick up the
environmental bill.

## The Math of Discontent

Is globalisation fundamentally bad? No. Taken as a whole, open trade has boosted
living standards, offered endless consumer choice, and powered the sharpest drop
in extreme global poverty in human history.

Yet the math of the backlash is simple: spread-out perks cannot soothe
concentrated pain. Saving ten dollars on a toaster or twenty dollars on a winter
coat means nothing to a family whose local factory shut down, whose wages
stalled for twenty years, and whose town lost its future.

The lesson of the past three decades isn't that we should pull up the drawbridge
and hide behind tariff walls. It is that globalisation cannot survive on
textbook fantasies. If governments fail to invest in serious retraining, fair
safety nets, worker protections, and resilient supply chains, voters will
inevitably dismantle the open world that made them prosperous.
